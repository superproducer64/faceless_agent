### Adapted Script

The most important lesson Quibi ever taught the industry is being misread — and it is costing producers real money.

The received wisdom is that Quibi proved audiences don't want vertical drama. That is wrong. What Quibi proved is that audiences won't pay a subscription fee to watch vertical drama on a siloed app with no social sharing. That is a distribution failure, not a format failure. And the conditions Quibi lacked — social-native distribution, zero-friction access, built-in virality — are now the default infrastructure of TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and an entire ecosystem of short-drama apps generating nine-figure annual revenues.

So if you've been sitting on the fence about vertical dramatic shorts because Quibi died, you've been solving the wrong problem.

[BRIDGE LINE — NEW RECORD REQUIRED] Here is what the format actually pays.

Based on producer interviews published in Variety, The Wrap, and Deadline across 2024, English-language commissions from these platforms are paying approximately $2,000 to $8,000 per episode. Run the maths. At 60 episodes per series: a $2,000 per-episode commission gives you a $120,000 series budget. At $8,000 per episode with 80 episodes, you're at $640,000. The per-minute rate is actually comparable to — and sometimes better than — conventional low-budget short-form TV. The problem is not the rate. The problem is pace.

### Adapted Script

Be honest with yourself about what you want from this format.

If your goal is CV-building — programmer attention, critical recognition, festival sales — the vertical dramatic short is not your primary vehicle right now. The absence of a competitive vertical section at Cannes, Venice, or Berlin is not an oversight. It reflects how those institutions have assessed the format's position in the prestige ecosystem. Tribeca's Storyscapes section exists and has screened vertical work, but it is curated around immersive and interactive work — not narrative drama in the conventional sense.

That may change. Festival programming follows money and cultural attention with a lag, and the short-drama app revenues are large enough that a programmer at a major festival will eventually need to account for the format. But that shift has not happened yet, and you should not structure a project around the assumption that it will on your timeline.

If your goal is revenue and production volume — building a lean, repeatable production operation with reliable commissions — the Chinese-model app ecosystem offers the clearest path. The prestige ceiling is low. The commercial floor is real.

### Adapted Script

Before you sign anything with a short-drama platform, understand what you are and are not selling.

The Chinese-model short drama platforms primarily operate on a work-for-hire basis. You are being paid to produce content, not to develop and license IP. That means the platform owns the series, the characters, the format, and typically all adaptation rights. You are a service provider with a production fee. That is not automatically a problem — plenty of sustainable production businesses are built on exactly that model — but it is a fundamentally different business than developing IP you can sell into multiple windows.

The question you need to answer before choosing your entry point is this: is this format a revenue stream for your production company, or a development opportunity for your IP slate? If it's the former, work-for-hire at volume may be exactly what you want. If it's the latter, you need a deal structure that preserves your rights — and those deals exist, but they are not the default.
