## Content Gaps

- No video addresses the vertical drama format from an **independent producer's business perspective** — specifically: who is buying, what the deal terms look like, what IP rights are retained, and whether the economics make sense relative to conventional short-form or low-budget feature production.
- The **Chinese-owned short drama app ecosystem** (ReelShort, DramaBox, FlexTV, Mango TV international) is covered as a consumer phenomenon but never explained as a **commissioning and supply-chain opportunity** for English-language independent producers. The work-for-hire vs co-production structures, episode counts (typically 60–100 episodes of 60–90 seconds), and the aggressive production pace these platforms require are unexplored in English-language producer-facing content.
- No competitor addresses the **post-Quibi landscape**: what Quibi's failure actually proved about the format (it was a distribution and UX failure, not a format failure), and which vertical-native platforms subsequently emerged or survived (Snap Originals, YouTube Shorts Fund, Meta's early scripted investment, dedicated short-drama apps).
- The **talent and union angle** is entirely absent from existing content: specifically whether SAG-AFTRA, BECTU, or Equity have low-budget or new-media agreements that cover vertical scripted production, and what happens when you try to attach experienced cast to a format with no established rate card.
- No competitor provides a **budget framework**: what a single 90-second dramatic episode actually costs to produce at professional quality vs amateur quality, what the per-minute rate looks like relative to conventional TV, and whether platforms are paying production fees that cover costs or expecting producers to subsidise the format.
- The **festival and prestige route** for vertical drama (Tribeca's vertical programme, Venice's Biennale College experiments, dedicated vertical film festivals) is unexplored in any producer-facing video — leaving the question of whether vertical drama can build a filmmaker's CV or is purely a commercial/platform exercise entirely unanswered.

## Key Data Points

- **ReelShort (Crazy Maple Studio) revenue:** ReelShort reportedly surpassed $100 million in cumulative in-app purchase revenue by late 2023 and was generating approximately $10–15 million per month by mid-2024, making it the highest-grossing short drama app in the US App Store. (Sources: data.ai / App Annie estimates cited in The Hollywood Reporter and Bloomberg, 2024.) *Flag: exact monthly figures shift rapidly; script should treat as ballpark and cite the trajectory rather than a precise number.*
- **Episode economics on Chinese-model short drama apps:** English-language productions commissioned for these platforms typically run 60–80 episodes per series at 60–90 seconds per episode. Reported per-episode production budgets for English-language commissions from platforms like ReelShort range from approximately $2,000–$8,000 per episode (total series budgets therefore $120,000–$640,000 for a full run). Producers report that the higher end is achievable only with lean crews and pre-existing infrastructure. (Sources: producer interviews in Variety, The Wrap, and Deadline, 2024; specific figures are from named producers who have worked with these platforms.) *Flag: these figures are directionally reliable but not publicly confirmed by platforms themselves.*
- **Quibi's format data before shutdown:** Quibi launched April 2020, shut down December 2020 after failing to reach its 7.4 million subscriber target (it reached approximately 500,000 paid subscribers). However, the "Turnstyle" format (content that reoriented for portrait or landscape automatically) was praised by creators. Key lesson: Quibi failed due to the absence of social sharing mechanics and a subscription paywall during a period when free content was abundant — not because audiences rejected vertical drama. (Source: Quibi investor letters, CEO Meg Whitman's post-mortem statements, multiple trade press analyses 2020–2021.)
- **Snap Originals commissioning:** Snapchat has commissioned several hundred Snap Originals since 2018, a proportion of which are scripted drama series in vertical format. Snap has reported over 30 million viewers for top Originals. The platform moved away from exclusive originals around 2023 in favour of licensed and creator-produced content, but has not exited scripted vertical entirely. Snap's content budget peaked around $100 million annually and was substantially reduced post-2022. (Sources: Snap Inc. investor relations filings, Variety coverage of Snap Originals slate.)
- **SAG-AFTRA New Media Agreement coverage:** SAG-AFTRA's New Media Agreement (most recently updated in 2023 as part of the broader 2023 TV/Theatrical negotiations) technically covers content produced for streaming and digital platforms, including short-form. However, the minimum scale rates for "new media" productions with budgets under specific thresholds are lower than television minimums. Productions for platforms like TikTok or Instagram with no upfront licence fee may fall outside standard coverage, creating ambiguity. BECTU (UK) has no specific vertical format rate card as of early 2025. (Source: SAG-AFTRA New Media Agreement 2023 text; BECTU published rate cards.)
- **Tribeca Festival vertical programme:** Tribeca has included a dedicated "Storyscapes" immersive and interactive section since 2012, and has screened vertical-format narrative work. However, there is no standalone competitive section for vertical dramatic shorts at Tribeca or at Cannes/Venice/Berlin as of the knowledge cutoff. The Tribeca X (branded content) strand has included vertically shot commercial work. The absence of a major A-list festival section for vertical drama is itself a significant data point about how the prestige film world has not yet institutionalised the format. *Flag: this landscape may have shifted by 2026; flag in script as "as of early 2025."*

## Recommended Angle

The video should position itself as the **industry intelligence brief that no one else has written**: a clear-eyed, non-hype assessment of vertical dramatic short form as a business opportunity for the working independent producer in 2026. The angle is not "vertical drama is the future of storytelling" (too breathless) nor "vertical drama is a distraction" (too dismissive) — it is: **"Here is exactly what the market looks like, who is paying, what they are paying, what it costs to produce, and what strategic decision you need to make."** The channel's authoritative, dry, insider tone is the differentiator — this audience does not need to be sold on the format's existence, they need a producer's-level breakdown of the commissioning landscape (Chinese-model apps vs Western platforms vs festival/prestige routes), a realistic budget framework per episode, the union and talent complications, the IP ownership question (work-for-hire vs retained rights), and an honest verdict on whether this belongs in a producer's slate as a primary project or a strategic side bet. The video should explicitly address the Quibi lesson not as a cautionary tale but as a corrective to a widespread misreading: Quibi proved that vertical drama needs social distribution mechanics and free or low-friction access — which is exactly what TikTok, Reels, and the short-drama apps now provide. That reframe is the intellectual hook that distinguishes this from every existing piece of content on the subject.
